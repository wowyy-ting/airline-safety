Research questions

These questions came out of our first look at the dataset. Many reach beyond what the spreadsheet can answer on its own, which is why the project pairs the data with scholarly sources.

  1. How have advancements in aviation technology and safety regulations influenced airline safety between 1985 and 2014?
  2. How do differences in government regulations and safety standards across countries affect the safety records of airlines?
  3. How does an airline’s history of accidents influence public trust and passengers’ willingness to fly with that airline?
  4. How has media coverage of major airline accidents shaped public perceptions of airline safety, regardless of actual accident rates?
  5. What role do economic pressures and competition between airlines play in decisions regarding maintenance, staffing, and passenger safety?
  6. How do differences in labor laws and working conditions across countries influence airline safety, particularly regarding pilot fatigue, working hours, and mandatory rest periods? To what extent might these differences help explain regional patterns in aviation accidents?
  7. How do airlines operating in regions with challenging weather conditions, such as Southeast Asia’s monsoon seasons or Northern Europe’s snowy winters, compare in terms of accident patterns, and what does this suggest about their ability to adapt to environmental risks?
  8. How do airlines respond to fatal accidents, and what do their responses reveal about corporate responsibility and accountability?
  9. How do accident patterns differ between airlines operating Boeing and Airbus aircraft, and what factors should be considered before concluding that one manufacturer is safer than the other?
  10. How has the distribution of aircraft manufacturers changed over time, particularly with the dominance of Boeing and Airbus?
  11. What patterns can be observed in airlines’ transitions between manufacturers and how this relates to the number of accidents, and how has the representation of other commercial aircraft manufacturers evolved throughout the dataset?
  12. How might the exclusion of smaller commercial airlines create an incomplete picture of aviation safety, particularly in regions where smaller carriers play a significant role in air transportation?
  13. How have historical events, such as economic recessions, political conflicts, and international crises, influenced airline safety during the periods represented in the dataset?
  14. How does the amount of aviation accident data available across different regions, such as North America, Europe, APAC, LATAM, and MEA, reflect potential geographic biases in data collection?
  15. How have airlines used their safety records in advertising and branding to build consumer confidence, and how accurately do these messages reflect their actual safety performance?

Data critique

How the data was generated

Our dataset focuses on airline safety for 56 airlines from 1985–2014. It includes the number of incidents, fatal accidents, and total fatalities for each airline. The data is divided into two 15-year periods: 1985–1999 and 2000–2014. Available seat kilometers flown per week are also included, which shows the size and popularity of an airline. This data was used through aviation accident and incident records from the Aviation Safety Network (ASN), which provides standards for how incidents and accidents are documented and classified. The ASN follows definitions established by the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) to distinguish accidents from incidents, and also records information from official investigation reports. An accident is an event involving an aircraft that results in serious or fatal injuries, significant aircraft damage, or an aircraft going missing. An incident is an event that affects or could affect flight safety but does not meet the criteria of an accident, like a near collision or small mechanical failures. Fatalities are deaths resulting from an accident, including those occurring within 30 days due to injuries sustained. One thing to note is that these classifications determine what gets included or excluded from the data. (Ex. Minor operational issues and passenger medical emergencies are excluded.) This can largely affect how the public interprets airline safety.

Original sources

The source of our GitHub dataset is a publicly accessible website called Aviation Safety Network (ASN), which is operated by the Flight Safety Foundation (FSF). The Flight Safety Foundation is an international nonprofit organization founded in 1945 by Jerome F. “Jerry” Lederer, Gloria Heath, and other aviation safety advocates. The ASN website contains three major databases: ASN Accident, ASN Wikibase, and ASN Drone. The ASN Accident database collects information from official government sources, accident investigation boards, and civil aviation authorities. The website is updated daily and contains over 23,000 aviation accident and incident records dating back to 1919. These records cover commercial aircraft, defined by ASN as aircraft certified to carry 12 or more passengers, as well as military transport aircraft and corporate jets. For each aviation accident or incident, ASN provides detailed information and cites its original sources at the bottom of the record. These sources often include organizations such as the U.S. National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB), the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), and established news outlets such as The New York Times. This allows users to trace the information back to its sources and assess its reliability. For our project, we will be using the ASN Accident database.

What the spreadsheet leaves out

The airline safety dataset that we are working with tells us about incidents, fatal accidents, and fatalities across 56 airlines. Still, it leaves out some important factors that could help our team explain differences in airline safety. One significant limitation that our team recognized was the lack of the total number of flights or passengers carried by each airline in the two time periods. It does include available seat kilometers per week, but this measure doesn’t provide a full picture of each airline’s operations throughout the 30 years. Larger airlines are going to service more frequent flights, meaning that they could experience more incidents as they have more frequent exposure to risk. Without this information, it would be difficult to tell whether an airline with more incidents is actually less safe than other ones with fewer incidents. Also, our dataset does not explain the cause or circumstances surrounding each incident. Factors such as weather conditions, mechanical failures, or air traffic control could all contribute to these incidents, but none are represented in the dataset. The data also excludes the geographic location of the incidents, which would be valuable insight on different geographic operating environments and regional airline information.

Another limitation we have seen is the grouping of two 15-year time periods instead of a clear yearly breakdown; this makes it slightly more difficult to identify specific years when the safety might have drastically increased or decreased based on regulations and other safety factors. Therefore, these omissions in data tell us that the number of incidents alone doesn’t necessarily tell us the full story of airline safety. Our group will spend significant time researching other social and political factors occurring around the world in the two time periods of data that we are working with.

The dataset’s ontology

FiveThirtyEight organizes the data into categories such as incidents, fatal accidents, and fatalities, encouraging us to judge airlines based on these numbers alone rather than the full story. It is formatted to influence the way we understand or compare airline safety. An airline with more incidents might seem less safe, even though they just operate more flights than smaller airlines. The choice of categories and data displayed can shape a narrative or mislead interpretations. It leaves out important factors that influence safety, like weather conditions, pilot error, political tensions, and differences in operating sizes and regulations. The dataset also groups information into two 15-year periods, overlooking improvements or changes that could have happened within individual years. If this dataset were our only source, we would miss more personal experiences from passengers, specific outlines of ‘incidents’, and the circumstances that surround them. We are encouraged to judge these airlines based on numerical data rather than understanding the full story, which is what we want to work towards refining.
